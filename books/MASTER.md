# CHAPTER 2 — THE MACHINE: IBM AND THE HOLLERITH CONTRACT

Every name on every transport list from every European concentration camp ran through a tabulator built by the International Business Machines Corporation of Endicott, New York. The machines were leased, not sold. A service engineer came monthly from the Berlin subsidiary to repair them.

## 2.1 What is a Hollerith machine

In 1884, Herman Hollerith, a German-American, patented an electromechanical device. It read holes punched into stiff cards (7-3/8 by 3-1/4 inches). It sorted, counted, and tabulated them faster than any human clerk. He used it for the 1890 United States Census. The company he founded, the Tabulating Machine Company, merged in 1911 with three other firms. This became the Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company (CTR). In 1924, under Thomas J. Watson Sr., CTR was renamed the International Business Machines Corporation—IBM.

A Hollerith machine is not a computer. It is a sorter. It has no memory, no logic. It reads a card. It directs the card to a bin based on the holes present. But it is fast. A single installation of Hollerith tabulators, with a trained staff, can process millions of records per month.

In the 1930s, the most advanced Hollerith installations on the European continent were owned and operated by IBM's wholly owned German subsidiary, Deutsche Hollerith Maschinen Gesellschaft m.b.H.—Dehomag, based in Berlin.

Dehomag had one dominant customer. That customer was the government of Germany.

## 2.2 The 1933 German census

On April 12, 1933, two and a half months after Adolf Hitler became Chancellor, the German government announced a national census. It would be conducted on June 16, 1933. The scope expanded. Detailed occupational data, and critically, religious affiliation data, were collected for every resident of the Reich.

The tabulation was awarded, without competitive bid, to Dehomag.

Willy Heidinger, Dehomag's managing director, was an enthusiastic Nazi. He gave the contract address to Dehomag's Berlin staff on January 8, 1934. The printed text of his speech survives in the IBM corporate archive. He said:

"We are recording the individual characteristics of each single member of the nation onto a little card... We are proud that we may assist in such a task which provides our nation's Physician."

The Physician meant the Führer.

"Our Physician can then determine whether the calculated values are in harmony with the health of our people."

I know these receipts.

# CHAPTER 5 — PAPERCLIP: HOW NAZI SCIENTISTS BUILT AMERICAN ROCKETRY

## 5.1 The program

May 5, 1945. The Third Reich was collapsing. The United States Joint Chiefs of Staff issued a secret directive. They authorized the Joint Intelligence Objectives Agency (JIOA) to identify German scientists. The goal: technical knowledge useful to American military and industrial development. The program’s internal code name was **OVERCAST**.

September 3, 1946. President Harry S. Truman formally authorized the program. It was renamed **Operation Paperclip**—after the paperclips used to attach the security dossiers to the JIOA's recruitment applications.

Paperclip ran from 1945 through 1959. I looked at the JIOA's declassified records (National Archives, Record Group 330, Records of the Office of the Secretary of Defense). The program brought to the United States: approximately 1,600 German and Austrian scientists, engineers, and technicians. Three thousand seven hundred dependent family members. The stated U.S. government purpose was simple: secure German technical talent for the military-industrial apparatus. Deny that talent to the Soviet Union, which ran its own program, **Osoaviakhim**. ## 5.2 The JIOA's file laundering

## G.8 The graph

The software’s function is not mysterious. I will describe it in one paragraph, for the technical reality determines the political reality.

A knowledge graph is a data structure. Entities—persons, organizations, financial transactions, vehicles, locations, events—are modeled as nodes. Relationships between them—employment, ownership, contact, co-travel, transaction—are modeled as edges. The graph is not static. It updates incrementally as new data sources are ingested. A query is not a traditional database lookup. It is a path-finding operation through the relational structure. It returns, for a seed node, all nodes within a specified number of hops, filtered by edge types, with weights assigned by relevance.

Palantir's Gotham and Foundry platforms make this operationally feasible. They allow a federal agency, ICE for example, to ingest into a single unified knowledge graph:

## G.9 What the ledger was

The system the knowledge graph replaced operated in analog. The scale was approximately six orders of magnitude smaller. The human labor was six orders of magnitude greater.

The pre-graph American enforcement state operated on ledgers. Card-catalogs. Paper files. Alphabetized index systems. An agent seeking to investigate a subject traversed these ledgers serially.

A request for records went to agency A. It returned a file in ninety days. That file was cross-referenced against agency B's catalog. That took an additional sixty days. I paid a commercial records-research firm for state DMV records. That took two weeks. Cell-phone records required a court-approved subpoena to a specific carrier. That took a month. It returned thirty days of call-detail records on a thermal-fax roll.

The total elapsed time from investigation initiation to a multi-source cross-referenced profile was, in the 1980s and early 1990s, six months to a year per subject. The investigator cost, at federal pay scales, of producing such a profile was one investigator-year.

The ledger was slow. Fragmentary. Labor-intensive. Three properties combined to form a functional privacy infrastructure. The cost of investigating one person was high. The number of subjects for systematic investigation was therefore bounded by the number of investigators. An enforcement agency with 5,000 sworn agents could maintain roughly 5,000 active substantial investigations. That was the operational ceiling.

The knowledge graph removes the ceiling.

The ICE Gotham instance in 2025 does not need 5,000 investigators to maintain investigations on 5,000 subjects. It maintains profiles on every record, continuously. Alerts fire when defined conditions are met. The investigator’s role shifts. It moves from producing a profile through labor to triaging the graph’s outputs through judgment. One investigator, with graph assistance, maintains awareness of thousands of subjects.

The math changes. The operational ceiling on enforcement changes. The privacy infrastructure—which was the ledger’s slowness—is removed.
