The Story

The Registry

Mystery / Thriller / Family

Regina "Nonna" Russo is fifty-two, a grandmother, and a former criminal defense investigator who spent thirty years helping people the system had failed. Now she runs a bookstore and minds her own business.

When her bridge club friend Doris Kowalski disappears from Vista del Mar Estates — a gated golf community where the grass is always greener and the secrets are buried beneath perfectly trimmed sod — Regina is pulled back into the work she thought she'd left behind.

Doris wasn't just a retired bookkeeper. She was building a case. Forty years of records. A wrongful conviction from 1987. A daughter seeking justice for a father who died in prison for crimes he didn't commit.

Helping Regina is Danny, her thirty-nine-year-old son covered in tattoos from years inside, the "conspiracy-minded" son who sees patterns where others see noise. Hindering her is David, her forty-five-year-old corporate lawyer son who wants her to let the police handle it.

The antagonist is Victor Harcourt, seventy, CEO of Pacific Meridian Holdings — refined, charitable, and utterly ruthless. He has spent decades hiding behind shell companies and legal teams. He has never faced a grandmother who knows where to hide a gun in her kitchen.

This is a novel about family, justice, and the hard-won wisdom that the difficult path is sometimes the only path. It's propulsive, impossible to put down, and ends exactly the way it should: with the antagonist getting what he deserves, and a family finally seeing each other clearly.

This is the advantage of being a grandmother — nobody expects the Camry to contain violence until the Camry contains violence.

— THE REGISTRY

The Characters

Regina "Nonna" Russo

Protagonist / Investigator / Grandmother

Fifty-two. Warm but world-weary. Dry humor. Loves her difficult sons more than they know. Can't stop giving a shit even when it's easier not to. Talks to her dead husband. Makes bad coffee and drinks it anyway.

Danny Russo

The Son Who Sees Patterns

Thirty-nine. Covered in tattoos from years inside. The "conspiracy-minded" son who was right more often than anyone wanted to admit. Desperately loves his mother but doesn't know how to say it without sounding crazy. Finally gets to prove his value.

David Russo

The Corporate Lawyer

Forty-five. Straight arrow. The "good" son who never understood why his mother cared about "those people." Calls every Sunday. Learns, eventually, to be proud of the mother who never stopped fighting.

Maeve

The Mother / The Network

Seventy-eight. Bridge club queen. Knows everything, says little. Lemon trees in violation of HOA rules. The tone that says "I know you're lying, I love you anyway."

Victor Harcourt

The Antagonist

Seventy. CEO of Pacific Meridian Holdings. Refined. Charitable. Has spent decades stealing from widows and framing the powerless. Gets his in the end. Publicly. Humiliatingly.

Vista del Mar Estates. The kind of place where the grass is always greener, and the secrets are always buried just beneath the perfectly trimmed sod.

— THE SETTING

The Voice

The Registry is written in what we call "swept-rug prose" — prose that guides you along like a steady walk, then suddenly the ground isn't there, but the momentum carries you through the fall and you land running.

The voice blends Lee Child's deceptive simplicity (short sentences that walk you toward trapdoors), Charlie Huston's kinetic velocity (street-level chaos that sweeps you along), Michael Connelly's procedural precision, Sue Grafton's first-person warmth, and Stephen King's character depth.

The result is propulsive, impossible to put down, and deeply satisfying — a mystery that moves like a thriller but lands like a family story.
