REMEMBERING 
DON "DUCK" EDWING 
1934 — 2016 


Duck Edwing embodied everythins a MAD reader would expect when meeting one 
of the "Usual Gang of Idiots.” He was a larger-than-life personality and delivered a 
constant barrage of corny jokes, tortured puns and botched magic tricks. 


Ca 


Not a day would go by without him calling in with his latest joke. He wouldn't even say 
hello, he would just launch right into the joke — and | would put him on speaker phone 
so the entire staff could hear. When he was finished, no matter how funny the joke, we 
would always react the same way: with complete silence. Then Duck would hang up. 


In the pre-email days, he would also keep the MAD fax machine humming all day 
with his ideas. Many were hilarious and wound up in the pages of the magazine. 
Occasionally he would send in something completely filthy and unpublishable with 
“Just for the boys...” written across it.

Duck in his studio > Photo: Greg Preston 


We travelled together on many MAD trips, from Italy to Switzerland to Bermuda — 
and everyone always wanted to be around Duck. He was genuine, warm and 
funny. On our trip to Switzerland, we were all in a cable car, slowly making our 
way up the mountain, and Duck was in rare form, letting fly a slew of bad one- 
liners to his captive audience. After the groans had stopped, from the back of the 
car came Al Jaffee’s voice with one of my favorite quips of all time: “You know, 
| just realized, Duck Edwing is a verb!” 


Over the course of his MAD career, Duck contributed many cover ideas, wrote and 
illustrated dozens of articles and Spy vs Spy episodes. He also wrote many Don 
Martin gags for which he was not credited at the time of publication. 


Duck Edwing was a terrific, talented man and will be terribly missed by MAD fans 
and all who knew him.

REMEMBERING 
PAUL PETER PORGES 
1927 — 2016 


You always knew when Paul Peter Porges was visiting MAD. He would methodically work 
his way down the hallway of individual offices, stopping at each, pounding on the door 
with his fist while bellowing “PASSPORTS!” He did this shtick for years, but it never got old. 


Like many of the characters he drew, “PPP,” as he was known to many, was always kind 
of rumpled and misshapen. In the winter, he wore a full-length down coat which fit him like 
a blanket fits a restless sleeper. Unlike Duck Edwing, he despised jokes, especially puns. 
But just like Duck, being in his presence meant you were in for some serious laughter. He 
had a rubbery face that he could contort instantly into a host of outrageous expressions. 
Physical and imposing, he loved to mimic actors from old movies. His favorite was Peter 


Paul Peter Porges and Tom Bunk 


Lorre from Casablanca. 


Paul and Lucie Porges 


Paul Peter had a very difficult early life. As a boy he fled his Austrian homeland to avoid 
the Nazis. (As payback, Nazis were often the punch line to some of PPP's most pointed 
jabs.) He had many brushes with death. While in France, he avoided deportation by hiding 
with the trash. Later, he illegally crossed into Switzerland. Because he was under 16, the 
Swiss did not send him back to the Nazis. After the war, he attended the Academy of Art in 
Geneva, where he met his future wife, also from Vienna, Lucie. Theirs was one of the great 
improbable love affairs. As loud and gregarious as Paul Peter was, Lucie was elegant and 
refined. She would delight at watching him entertain the MAD staff with his wild antics. 
And then, with the simple phrase, “Okay PPP, that is enough,” she would rein him in. 


As a reader, I loved PPP's writing when paired with Paul Coker's artwork. But only later, 
when PPP started illustrating his own ideas, did his true absurdist wit burst forth onto the 
pages of MAD. It takes a special kind of twisted mind to imagine what it was like to play 
shuffleboard on Columbus’ ship, the Santa Maria (MAD #223), or dream up “Other Uses 
for Live Lobsters” (reprinted in this issue's "MAD Vault"). 


Whenever | saw PPP he never failed to ask about my daughter, Mary Emily. A father 
himself to two daughters, Vivette and Claudia, family was always paramount to PPP. He 
was a wonderful member of the dysfunctional MAD family. 


— John Ficarra, MAD Executive Editor
